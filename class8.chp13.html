1. FORMULAS & RULES

Sample Space and Outcomes

Sample Space:
S = Set of all possible outcomes
Number of Sample Points:
n(S) = Total number of possible outcomes
Two Coin Tosses:
S = {HH, HT, TH, TT}
n(S) = 4
Three Coin Tosses:
n(S) = 2³ = 8
Two Dice:
n(S) = 6 × 6 = 36
One Die and One Coin:
n(S) = 6 × 2 = 12
Two Spinners with 3 Outcomes Each:
n(S) = 3 × 3 = 9
General Counting Rule:
Total outcomes = Product of the number of choices at each stage
Repeated Two-Outcome Trials:
Number of possible outcomes = 2ⁿ
(When each trial has exactly 2 possible outcomes)

Probability

Probability of an Event:
P(E) = n(E) / n(S)
Favourable Outcomes:
P(E) = Number of favourable outcomes / Total number of equally likely outcomes
Probability Range:
0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1
Impossible Event:
P(E) = 0
Sure or Certain Event:
P(E) = 1
Complementary Event:
P(E′) = 1 − P(E)
Complement Rule:
P(E) + P(E′) = 1
Experimental Probability:
P(E) = Number of times event occurs / Total number of trials
Theoretical Probability:
P(E) = Number of favourable outcomes / Total number of equally likely outcomes

Independent and Dependent Events

Independent Events:
P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)
Three Independent Events:
P(A and B and C) = P(A) × P(B) × P(C)
Dependent Events:
P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B | A)
Mutually Exclusive Events:
A ∩ B = ∅
Mutually Exclusive Probability Rule:
P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B)
Exhaustive Events:
A ∪ B ∪ C ∪ ... = S
Event and Sample Space:
0 ≤ n(E) ≤ n(S)

2. CONCEPTS

Representing Sample Space

  • Listing Outcomes: Write all possible outcomes inside set brackets.
  • Two-Dimensional Grid: Use a row and a column to show combinations of outcomes from two experiments.
  • Tree Diagram: Use branches to show possible outcomes at each stage.
  • Each possible outcome must be included without unnecessary repetition.
  • For independent stages, multiply the number of choices at each stage to find the total number of outcomes.

Types of Events

  • Simple Event: Contains one sample point.
  • Compound Event: Contains more than one sample point.
  • Equally Likely Events: Events with equal chances of occurring.
  • Independent Events: The occurrence of one event does not change the probability of the other.
  • Dependent Events: The occurrence of one event changes the probability of the other.
  • Mutually Exclusive Events: Events that cannot occur together in the same trial.
  • Exhaustive Events: Events whose combined outcomes cover the entire sample space.
  • Complementary Event: The event that the original event does not occur.

With and Without Replacement

  • With Replacement: The selected item is returned before the next selection. In ordinary random draws, this keeps the probabilities unchanged and makes successive draws independent.
  • Without Replacement: The selected item is not returned. The probabilities for later draws may change, making the draws dependent.

Probability Types

  • Experimental Probability: Based on the results of actual trials.
  • Theoretical Probability: Based on possible outcomes under an assumed probability model.
  • Experimental probability may differ from theoretical probability, especially when the number of trials is small.
  • With many repeated trials, experimental probability may approach theoretical probability under suitable conditions.

3. DEFINITIONS

Probability: A numerical measure of the likelihood that an event will occur, ranging from 0 to 1, or 0% to 100%.

Experiment: An action or process that produces one or more well-defined outcomes.

Trial: A single performance or repetition of an experiment.

Outcome: The result of a single trial in an experiment.

Random Experiment: An experiment repeated under similar conditions that may produce different outcomes.

Sample Space: The set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment, usually denoted by S.

Sample Point: An individual outcome belonging to the sample space.

Event: A set of outcomes of a random experiment whose probability is being considered.

Simple Event: An event containing exactly one sample point.

Compound Event: An event containing more than one sample point.

Equally Likely Events: Events that have equal probabilities of occurring.

Independent Events: Events in which the occurrence of one does not affect the probability of the other.

Dependent Events: Events in which the occurrence of one affects the probability of the other.

Mutually Exclusive Events: Events that have no common outcomes and cannot occur together in the same trial.

Exhaustive Events: A collection of events whose union is the entire sample space.

Complementary Event: The event consisting of all outcomes in which the original event does not occur.

Experimental Probability: Probability calculated from the observed results of repeated trials.

Theoretical Probability: Probability calculated from the possible equally likely outcomes of an experiment.

4. KEY POINTS

  • Probability can be written as a fraction, decimal or percentage.
  • Probability always lies between 0 and 1, inclusive.
  • A probability of 0 means impossible; a probability of 1 means certain.
  • A probability of 1/2 represents a 50:50 chance.
  • The sample space contains every possible outcome of an experiment.
  • The number of favourable outcomes cannot exceed the total number of outcomes.
  • For equally likely outcomes, divide the number of favourable outcomes by the total number of possible outcomes.
  • When a coin is tossed n times, the number of possible sequences is 2ⁿ.
  • When two dice are rolled, there are 36 ordered outcomes.
  • Mutually exclusive events have no common outcome: A ∩ B = ∅.
  • Mutually exclusive events are not necessarily exhaustive.
  • If events are both mutually exclusive and exhaustive, their combined outcomes cover the whole sample space without overlap.
  • With replacement usually makes successive random draws independent; without replacement usually makes them dependent.
  • For independent events, multiply their probabilities to find the probability that all occur.
  • For dependent events, use the conditional probability after the first event.
  • The complement of E is written E′ or Eᶜ.
  • The probabilities of an event and its complement add up to 1.
  • Experimental probability depends on observed results; theoretical probability depends on the probability model.
  • A larger number of trials can make experimental probability closer to theoretical probability, but does not guarantee an exact match.
  • Probability is useful in weather forecasting, finance, statistics, computer science and decision-making.
